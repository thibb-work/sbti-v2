04 · BASELINE

Measure from where you are now.

V2 moves the base year to your latest data, asks for limited assurance, and makes you name the emissions that actually move the needle.

CNZS-C4 · At the start of every five-year cycle, you move your base year forward to the most recent year with comprehensive data. This continues your decarbonization — it doesn't restart it: you still show progress cycle over cycle, and you can keep communicating against an earlier reference year too.

Assuring your base-year inventory · CNZS-C7:

CNZS-C6 — Alongside your inventory, you identify and quantify emissions-intensive activities (EIAs) across your value chain — using the SBTi's reference list. An EIA is significant if it represents 5% or more of your scope 3 emissions (categories 1–14), and you report both its absolute emissions and its share of the total. You can't assume an EIA is absent without doing the analysis — and where significant EIAs exist, your transition plan has to show how you'll decarbonize them.

CNZS-C8 — If your structure, boundaries, or activities change materially during a cycle (mergers, divestments, a 5% shift in any scope's emissions), you recalculate your base year inventory and update the metrics that depend on it.

05 · SCOPE 1

Scope 1: three routes to total coverage.

Near-term scope 1 targets must cover 100% of your direct emissions. Pick one or more of three routes — open any card for the detail.

C10.2a — A straight-line absolute emissions cut from your base year to residual levels by 2050. This is the only route where a long-term target stays optional: set the near-term target and you're compliant. Best suited to companies without an applicable sector pathway.

C10.2b — Align scope 1 to a published sector decarbonization pathway, measured as emissions per unit of physical output. A long-term target is mandatory, and your transition plan is due at Target Validation — no grace period.

C10.2c — New in V2. Instead of an emissions curve, you commit to a dated schedule to abate, retire or decommission specific emitting assets, set out in an Asset Decarbonization Plan with milestones and/or a carbon budget. Long-term target mandatory — designed for asset-heavy operators.

Routes can be mixed; if you set more than one, they aggregate into one scope 1 figure for communication only (C10.4).

Routes B and C lose the 15-month grace period — the transition plan publishes at Target Validation, not afterwards.

Where relevant, transition plans must commit to phasing out revenue from unabated fossil fuels, in line with net-zero pathways.

Bioenergy emissions are reported separately from removals — no netting — following GHG Protocol Land Sector & Removals guidance (LSRS), alongside FLAG and removals accounting (C5.5).

Definitive requirements: the published Standard, Chapter 3 (CNZS-C10).

06 · SCOPE 2

Scope 2: two ways to express the ambition.

Near-term scope 2 targets must cover 100% of your purchased electricity, heat, steam and cooling. For electricity buyers, V2 is less about whether instruments exist and more about where they can credibly be claimed.

TARGET OPTIONS · CNZS-C12

Choose the target expression, then design the contract stack.

Low-carbon electricity (LCE) alignment target C12.2a

Grow the share of low-carbon electricity you use, contract, or match on a linear path toward an eligible net-zero pathway. LCE now covers more than renewables: renewables, nuclear, and CCS-fitted generation all count. The long-term version reaches 100% LCE (C13.2a).

Category A companies also submit projected electricity consumption at validation. If projected demand growth exceeds 20% a year over the cycle, an emissions target becomes mandatory alongside or instead of the LCE target (C12.4).

Absolute emissions reduction target C12.2b

Cut scope 2 absolute emissions on a linear trajectory to a defined residual level, consistent with an eligible net-zero pathway. The long-term version lands at residual levels by 2050 (C13.2b). Long-term targets stay optional on this route, but Category A still files electricity demand projections at validation.

PROCUREMENT CLAIMS

EACs still count, but the claim changes.

EACs, including EECS Guarantees of Origin, are still eligible instruments under SBTi V2. What changes is the claim they back. They can back low-carbon electricity alignment and system-contribution claims — but not a physical emissions-reduction claim, unless your physical GHG inventory actually changes.

ActionStrongest claim
Energy efficiency reducing electricity usePhysical emissions reduction
On-site solar consumed on sitePhysical reduction and LCE alignment
Direct-line low-carbon electricityStrong activity-level LCE alignment
Grid-connected PPA in relevant activity poolLCE alignment / target implementation / possible system contribution
Unbundled EECS GO or EACLCE alignment / target implementation, if quality rules are met
Out-of-region certificates without deliverability evidenceWeak claim; likely not robust for SBTi V2

"We reduced emissions by buying EACs."

DELIVERABILITY REGIONS · CNZS-C30

Activity pools and deliverability: not always countries.

An activity pool is the electricity system that a company's load is connected to and can reasonably help decarbonise. For electricity, SBTi V2 points to deliverability regions, not simple political borders. A country can sometimes be a reasonable proxy, but not always.

ExampleWhy country is not enough
Sweden SE1 vs SE4Same country, different bidding zones and congestion.
Italy North vs Sicily/SardiniaSame country, different grid constraints and island/interconnection issues.
Denmark DK1 vs DK2Same country, separate electricity regions.
Great Britain vs Northern IrelandSame UK, different electricity systems.
United States: ERCOT, PJM, CAISO, MISOOne country, multiple grid and market regions.
Germany/LuxembourgCross-border market logic can matter more than country border.
Generic "Europe-wide GO portfolio"Too broad unless deliverability and allocation are documented.

Practical rule: Build the data model with site, country, bidding zone/grid zone, annual MWh, hourly data availability, and instrument origin. Do not just tag everything as "Europe".

Imports do not automatically validate neighbouring-country EACs.

Physical imports can matter for location-based grid factors. But a neighbouring-country EAC is not automatically valid for a consuming site just because electricity imports exist. For procurement claims, the instrument still needs to fit the relevant deliverability/activity-pool rules, or qualify under a specific flexibility.

HOURLY MATCHING · CNZS-C32-C34

Hourly matching is a procurement matching metric.

Hourly matching in SBTi V2 is not a pure location-based hourly emissions calculation. It is a procurement/attribute matching metric: the percentage of electricity consumption in significant activity pools that is used, contracted, or matched with low-carbon electricity on an hourly basis.

Threshold: The 10 GWh threshold applies per activity pool, not globally.

Activity poolAnnual electricity useHourly matching reporting?
France12 GWhYes
Italy8 GWhNo, unless part of a larger relevant activity pool
Germany50 GWhYes
Global total70 GWhNot the test by itself
Required · from 2027

Reporting your share is mandatory.

Under SBTi V2 (effective 1 February 2027), every activity pool using 10 GWh or more a year must calculate and report the percentage of electricity matched with low-carbon supply on an hourly basis (CNZS-C32). The number itself is not optional.

Optional · leadership

Hitting the thresholds is voluntary.

Meeting the 50 / 75 / 90% levels below earns nothing you are required to have. It only matters if you want SBTi to recognise you as a leader, on the Dashboard, under its optional Scope 2 Hourly Matching recognition program (CNZS-C33–C34).

Annual matching remains the core target implementation requirement. Hourly matching is mandatory to report for significant activity pools and optional for leadership recognition.

Target timing: don't reopen 2030 targets — get the next cycle ready instead.

If your 2030 targets are validated, deliver them. The real work for energy procurement is quietly preparing the 2030-2035 cycle. Five moves:

  1. Audit the contract stack. Map every PPA, vPPA, green tariff and EAC — and flag which ones still count post-2030.
  2. Read the load curves. Pull hourly consumption for the sites physically taking that power, and see how well today's instruments match it hour by hour.
  3. Find the gap. Wherever hourly matching breaks down is exactly the low-carbon supply you need to procure next.
  4. Start sourcing early. Lock deliverable, in-pool LCE before the next cycle — and the competition — forces your hand.
  5. Bring in backup. Loop in your procurement advisory team (Arcadia or similar) to execute while the window is open.

ENERGY PROCUREMENT ACTION CHECKLIST

What to do next.

  • Map electricity by site, country, bidding zone/grid zone, supplier and annual MWh.
  • Identify activity pools above 10 GWh/year.
  • Separate physical reduction levers from EAC/PPA implementation levers.
  • Build a legacy contract register before 1 February 2027.
  • Check generator age and commissioning/repowering date.
  • Require certificate serial numbers and cancellation evidence.
  • Require hourly generation and consumption data where available.
  • Review claim wording with legal/comms.
  • Avoid generic pan-European claims unless deliverability is documented.
  • Prioritise demand reduction, on-site/direct-line supply, and deliverable PPAs over cheap unbundled certificates.

These rules are easy to misapply on a first pass. Skip them until the basics above are solid.

PPA FLEXIBILITY · CNZS-C30.3.b

One PPA may cover multiple connected loads.

You can aggregate operations across interconnected activity pools in a wide-area synchronous grid if the aggregate load is covered by a PPA with a low-carbon electricity project in one of those pools, and offtake begins within 36 months of the project's commissioning.

So one new PPA may cover multiple connected loads instead of one per site or country — but only where the electricity systems are meaningfully interconnected and the PPA is tied to a recent low-carbon project.

Valid-ish

A company uses 5 GWh in Germany and 7 GWh in France. It signs a 12 GWh PPA with a new wind project in France. If the relevant regions are treated as interconnected and offtake begins within 36 months of commissioning, this may cover the aggregated load.

Not valid

A German load is covered by a PPA in Brazil. Different grid, no deliverability.

Not valid

A PPA starts offtake 5 years after project commissioning. This likely fails the 36-month condition.

LEGACY CONTRACTS · CNZS-C30.5

Grandfathering: build a legacy contract register before 1 February 2027.

Existing long-term contracts signed before the V2 effective date may be grandfathered for the life of the contract — but renewal does not extend the benefit. Document exactly which loads and activity pools each legacy contract covered before the effective date.

For every PPA, VPPA, green tariff, GO/EAC arrangement, capture:

  • Contract signature date
  • Start date and end date
  • Renewal rights
  • Generator commissioning or repowering date
  • Technology
  • Country, bidding zone, grid region and registry
  • Annual MWh
  • Sites/activity pools covered
  • Load-weighted allocation method
  • Certificate serial numbers and cancellation evidence
  • Hourly generation data availability
  • Claim wording allowed under the contract

CNZS-C23 · C30.4

Sector-level action is a fallback, not a cheap procurement route.

SBTi V2 allows sector-level action where local or activity-pool options are structurally constrained, for example because of regulation, infrastructure, market structure, immature certificate systems, or insufficient low-carbon electricity supply. Cost, internal preference, or weak procurement planning should not count.

Likely hard to justifyMore plausible
GermanySmall island grid with limited LCE supply
FranceJurisdiction with no credible EAC registry
SpainMarket where corporate PPAs are blocked or impractical due to regulation
NetherlandsActivity pool with demonstrably insufficient LCE supply during the target period

Even where sector-level action is used, companies should show they are also trying to enable local or regional low-carbon electricity development.

Definitive requirements: the published Standard, Chapter 3 (CNZS-C12) and Sections 4.3–4.4 (CNZS-C29–C34).

Scope 3 stops being a coverage rule. It becomes a decision system.

V1 asked one question — did you cover two-thirds of your value chain?

Scope 1 · Long-term Depends on your near-term route (C10.2)

  • Route A · Absolute contraction (C10.2a) — optional. A straight line to residual emissions by 2050 satisfies the Standard on its own; no separate long-term target is needed.
  • Route B · Sector pathway (C10.2b) — required. Aligning to a published sector pathway makes a long-term target mandatory, with the transition plan due at Target Validation — no grace period.
  • Route C · Asset decarbonization (C10.2c) — required. The dated abate / retire / decommission schedule must run all the way to a long-term target.

Scope 2 · Long-term Optional for all companies (C13.2)

A long-term scope 2 target is never mandatory. If you set one, the LCE route reaches 100% low-carbon electricity and the absolute route lands at residual levels by 2050. Category A companies still file electricity-demand projections at validation either way.

Scope 3 · Long-term Optional for all companies

Long-term scope 3 targets are optional for everyone and, where set, reach residual levels by 2050. The one case that forces a long-term scope 3 target is the category-11 escape hatch (C15.4): taking it commits you to a long-term category-11 target to residual levels by 2050 — see the fine print above.

Net-zero targets combine near- and long-term targets across all three scopes with neutralization of residuals at the target year — optional for everyone (C17).

Definitive requirements: the published Standard, Chapter 3 (CNZS-C14–C20).

08 · TARGET IMPLEMENTATION

Delivery is now a hierarchy.

V2 grades how you decarbonize, not just whether you do. Cut at source first. Every step down the ladder has to be earned by a demonstrated structural constraint — never preference, procurement choice, or cost alone.

  1. 1

    Activity level · CNZS-C21.1

    Cut at source.

    Reduce emissions inside your own operations and value chain — efficiency, fuel switching, behind-the-meter generation, and engaging suppliers and customers to cut theirs.

    Efficiency Fuel switching Behind-the-meter generation Supplier & customer engagement

    Earns Company-level claim — the reduction lands in your physical inventory.

  2. 2

    Activity pools · CNZS-C21.2 · C22

    Act within shared systems.

    Where emissions sit in systems you share — grids, supply sheds, logistics networks — act within them, at the smallest reasonable boundary. Market instruments may convey the low-carbon attribute: PPAs and EACs, biomethane, mass-balance and book-and-claim for materials.

    Grid & supply-shed action PPAs & EACs Mass balance Book & claim

    Earns System-contribution claim — separate from your inventory reduction (C24, C37.10.c).

  3. 3

    Sector level · CNZS-C23

    Act at sector level — only while constrained.

    Reach here only by demonstrating a structural constraint — region- or site-specific infrastructure, regulatory, market-structure or supply limits that block action higher up within the target timeframe. Sector action must be the same type of activity in a relevant geographic or system context.

    Documented structural constraint Same activity type Relevant geography

    Earns System-contribution claim — never a substitute for activity-level cuts.

Whatever rung you act on, instruments and projects must clear the integrity criteria — additionality, unique attribution, temporal alignment, no double counting (CNZS-C25–C26).

09 · PROVE IT

Say only what you can show.

Annual tracking, an end-of-cycle assessment, and three claim types that draw a clear line between reduction, alignment, and system contribution.

5 year cycle
Years 1–4 · Annual progress reports — emissions inventory, progress against targets, and any changes to your transition plan (C36).
Year 5 · End-of-Cycle Assessment — SBTi reviews five years of reported data against your targets and reissues a validation status (C37).

THREE CLAIM TYPES · CNZS-C37.10

What you can say publicly depends on what your End-of-Cycle Assessment shows. Three claim types, each with its own evidence bar.

Emissions-reduction claim · C37.10.a

Emissions reduction

The strongest claim — and the only basis for it is a change in your physical GHG inventory against your base year.

What you can say

"We reduced our scope 1 and 2 emissions by 14% against our base year, in line with our SBTi-validated target."

Alignment claim · C37.10.b

Net-zero alignment

Activity-level actions support a net-zero-alignment claim when they produce measurable change in your operations or value chain.

What you can say

"Our fleet electrification and supplier program are aligned with our SBTi-validated net-zero pathway."

System contribution claim · C37.10.c

System contribution

Activity-pool and sector-level actions sit outside your inventory, are reported separately, and only ever claim a contribution to decarbonizing the systems your value chain runs on.

What you can say

"Our book-and-claim purchases of near-zero steel contributed to decarbonizing the steel system our supply chain depends on."

C36.6 — If you miss an annual milestone, your progress report explains why, and what you're changing in your transition plan to get back on track. A single miss doesn't void your validation.

C37.8 — At the End-of-Cycle Assessment, SBTi weighs genuine best-efforts shortfalls (documented barriers, market constraints, force majeure) differently from inaction — but persistent, unexplained underperformance can lead to a status downgrade or removal from the target dashboard.

Definitive requirements: the published Standard, Chapter 5 (CNZS-C36–C37).

2035 AND BEYOND · CNZS-C45–C46

From 2035, this stops being voluntary: companies support eligible carbon removals covering at least 1% of ongoing emissions, rising linearly to 100% by their net-zero year — with a growing share of long-lived removals (C45). CNZS-C45

By 2050, your remaining emissions should be at residual levels — for most sectors, no more than about 10% of your base-year footprint — and you neutralize 100% of those residuals every year with eligible carbon removals (C46). OER between now and then is the on-ramp: it builds the removals market and the internal muscle you'll need at the finish line.

Definitive requirements: the published Standard, Chapter 6 (CNZS-C40, C45–C46) and Table 4.
